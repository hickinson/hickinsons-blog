# Editorial guide

This document defines the editorial baseline for hickinsons.blog.

It is intended for both human writing and AI-assisted drafting. It should guide decisions without turning the site into a rigid house style. The aim is coherence of voice with variety of form.

## What this site is

hickinsons.blog is an essay-led journal about thinking clearly, building useful things, understanding systems, and becoming more capable in a changing world.

The writing can move between work, technology, ideas and ordinary life. Coherence should come from the authorial voice and recurring questions, not from forcing every post into the same structure.

Useful recurring questions include:

- What is actually true here?
- What is worth building?
- What is the problem before the solution?
- What changes when technology changes the cost of producing an answer?
- What are people, teams or institutions really being asked to become capable of?
- What should remain unresolved?

## Voice

The preferred voice is thoughtful, calm, lightly technical where useful, human and quietly provocative.

Characteristic strengths to preserve:

- curious rather than performatively certain;
- practical rather than abstract for its own sake;
- attentive to systems, incentives, constraints and unintended consequences;
- willing to implicate the author rather than place every problem outside him;
- sceptical without becoming cynical;
- personal without becoming confessional for effect;
- comfortable allowing more than one thing to be true;
- willing to challenge a convenient framing without manufacturing outrage.

The aim is not to imitate surface features from earlier posts. Short paragraphs, questions, headings and aphoristic lines are techniques, not the voice itself.

## Choose the form before drafting

Do not default from idea to essay.

Before drafting, decide what kind of published object the material deserves to become. Valid forms include:

- pillar essay;
- reflective essay;
- field note;
- photo-led piece;
- reading note;
- list or notebook entry;
- signal response to another idea or source;
- letter;
- dialogue;
- unfinished question;
- creative or poetic piece.

"No full post yet" is also a valid editorial outcome. A thin observation should not be inflated into a long essay merely to satisfy a schedule.

When choosing a form, look at the previous four or five posts. Ask whether the proposed piece adds another texture to the journal or merely repeats the easiest recent structure.

## Paragraphs, headings and rhetorical devices

Single-sentence paragraphs are useful for emphasis. They should not become the default rhythm.

Use them deliberately rather than mechanically. A run of several single-sentence paragraphs should earn the effect.

Headings should help navigation, pacing or argument. They are not compulsory. A short or medium reflective piece may be stronger without them.

Use the following devices sparingly enough that they remain effective:

- question ladders;
- repeated "not X, but Y" constructions;
- "That matters" / "That distinction matters";
- several successive qualifications beginning "This is not...";
- aphoristic standalone lines;
- title phrases repeated in the final lines;
- endings beginning "Perhaps..." simply to sound reflective.

These are not banned. Repetition across the corpus is the concern.

## Concrete before universal

A lived event, work situation, book, conversation, photograph or practical problem should remain connected to the claim being made from it.

Do not move automatically from:

scene -> abstraction -> universal lesson.

A personal observation may remain personal. A photograph may remain a moment. A work problem may remain specific to its context.

Before generalising, ask whether the evidence actually supports the wider claim.

## Endings

Do not tie every post into a neat lesson.

An ending may:

- resolve the argument;
- return to the opening scene;
- leave a genuine question open;
- stop after the important moment;
- acknowledge uncertainty;
- simply finish once the point has been made.

A question-led ending is appropriate when the subject remains genuinely unresolved. Do not manufacture uncertainty for tone.

A conclusive ending is appropriate when the evidence supports a conclusion. Do not manufacture ambiguity merely to appear thoughtful.

A useful edit is to remove the final two paragraphs temporarily. If little or nothing important is lost, the piece may already have ended.

## Editorial hard gates

A draft is not ready for publication unless all of these are satisfied.

### Personal provenance

Never invent an experience, opinion, feeling, conversation, motivation, reaction or conclusion for the author.

If source material does not establish something, either omit it or make the uncertainty explicit.

### Factual provenance

Material factual claims must be verifiable, sourced where appropriate, or clearly framed as recollection, interpretation or opinion.

### Source integrity

Keep third-party material distinguishable from the author's interpretation. Do not blur quotation, paraphrase and personal view.

### Privacy and reputation

Check whether identifiable people, colleagues, organisations, private conversations or sensitive situations are being exposed unnecessarily.

### Copyright

Do not reproduce substantial third-party text merely because it exists in notes or captured source material. Quote only what is needed and add original interpretation.

### Article-worthiness

The system must be allowed to recommend holding, combining or reducing an idea rather than forcing it into a post.

### Technical validity

A publishable post must have valid MDX, required frontmatter, a sensible route and a successful site build.

### Release control

AI-assisted automation should stop at a reviewable draft pull request. Human review, revision and approval remain required before merge and publication.

## Editorial soft warnings

These are prompts to reconsider, not automatic failures.

- The same substantive form has been used repeatedly across recent posts.
- A short or medium piece has numerous conceptual headings.
- A large proportion of prose paragraphs contain only one sentence.
- Three or more single-sentence paragraphs appear consecutively without a clear reason.
- Several rhetorical questions appear in succession.
- Repeated antithesis constructions are doing more work than the underlying argument.
- Several paragraphs pass without returning to evidence, scene, example or practical consequence.
- A personal event is universalised without enough support.
- The draft explains its point again after already landing it.
- The final line echoes the title merely for symmetry.
- The ending creates artificial uncertainty.
- The ending creates artificial certainty.

## Categories

Primary categories should help readers understand what a piece is mainly about. They should operate at the same conceptual level.

The current primary taxonomy is:

- **Work** — organisations, leadership, public service, data and digital work, organisational change;
- **Technology** — AI, software, architecture, platforms and technical capability;
- **Ideas** — books, writing, language, society, philosophy and thinking;
- **Life** — family, home, sport, identity, personal development and lived experience.

These categories do not need equal numbers of posts. Balance should emerge from what the author is genuinely thinking about, not from writing to fill a bucket.

More specific concepts such as data, leadership, systems, building, AI, sport, family and reading are better treated as secondary themes or tags if that becomes useful.

"Building" remains an important recurring idea and part of the site's identity. It does not need to remain a primary filing category.

The homepage should not make category balance look like an editorial quota. A chronological journal view with optional category filtering is preferable to visually grouping the whole corpus by category by default.

Existing post categories should not be changed casually. Any future taxonomy change should be handled as a separate reviewed content change.

## Commonplace relationship

Essays and Commonplace entries serve different stages of thinking.

The blog is for ideas that have been developed into a publishable piece.

Commonplace is for things read, heard, saved and noticed while the thinking is still developing.

A public Commonplace item must include a reason for saving it. A source is not enough on its own.

Do not publish a bookmark dump. Prefer a short source excerpt or link plus the author's own note about why it matters, what it connects to or what remains unresolved.

## Automation principle

The purpose of automation is to reduce friction between captured thought and reviewable publication, not to manufacture output.

A useful pipeline is:

source material -> idea selection -> form decision -> evidence and provenance checks -> draft -> humanising/editing pass -> technical validation -> draft pull request -> human review.

The system should preserve variation, uncertainty and judgement rather than optimise every post towards the same polished essay shape.
