# Concrete detail can carry more than another theory paragraph

- **Status:** active
- **First observed:** 2026-08-28
- **Evidence:** PR #49; `src/mdx/a_few_days_somewhere_else.mdx`
- **Applies to:** drafting, reflective essays, travel writing, endings

## Context

The Scandinavia draft risked becoming a systems essay built from a short trip. The final piece kept the observations about transport, payment, cycling and public space but explicitly limited what four days could prove.

The Höllviken beach exchange — asking a local about jellyfish and getting the reply, “Not dangerous, but water is cold.” — then carried the piece into a more human register. The final edit also removed an extra closing line because the point had already landed.

## Learning

When a concrete, slightly odd or ordinary detail reveals the experience, do not replace it with another paragraph explaining the theme.

Keep proportion around what the evidence can support, and allow a specific exchange, image or practical detail to do argumentative work without immediately translating it into a universal lesson.

## Why this matters

Phil's writing becomes less generic when the systems observation remains attached to the actual experience that prompted it. The memorable detail is often where the voice lives; the theory is useful only if it helps the reader notice the same thing.

This also applies to endings. If a detail has already landed the point, an additional line written for symmetry or closure can make the piece feel more manufactured.

## When to apply

Retrieve this lesson for travel notes, photo-led pieces, personal observations and drafts where an unusual detail or short exchange is available in the source material.

## Do not overgeneralise

Not every anecdote is automatically meaningful, and not every piece should end on dialogue. The lesson is to preserve strong source detail and resist explaining it past the point where it has done its job.
