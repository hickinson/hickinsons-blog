# Human recognition beats an inferred curation score

- **Status:** active
- **First observed:** 2026-08-27
- **Evidence:** PR #48; Commonplace curation review
- **Applies to:** curation, Commonplace, idea selection

## Context

During a Commonplace clean-up, an automated review proposed removing `How to write` because it appeared comparatively thin. Phil said it still mattered to him, so it was retained.

The useful lesson was not that the automated review had failed mechanically. It had inferred importance from what was visible in the note and could not know the strength of Phil's continuing connection to the source.

## Learning

Do not treat textual depth, note length, apparent sophistication or model confidence as a proxy for whether a saved source still matters to Phil.

For personal curation, human recognition is the final signal. Automation can surface candidates to keep, remove, combine or revisit, but uncertainty about significance should be routed to Phil rather than resolved by scoring the artefact itself.

## Why this matters

The Commonplace and editorial inbox are not generic content databases. Their value depends on preserving the author's actual reasons for noticing and saving things, including reasons that may be lightly documented.

A system that optimises too aggressively for apparent substance will gradually replace a personal collection with one that merely looks well curated to an outsider.

## When to apply

Retrieve this lesson when pruning Commonplace entries, ranking editorial ideas, deduplicating saved material or deciding whether an apparently small capture is worth keeping.

## Do not overgeneralise

This does not mean nothing should ever be removed. Obsolete, duplicate or no-longer-meaningful material can and should be pruned. The point is that the model should not pretend it can infer personal significance from surface richness alone.
