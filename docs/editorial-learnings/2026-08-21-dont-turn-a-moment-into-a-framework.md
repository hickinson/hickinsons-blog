# Do not turn a small human observation into a framework

- **Status:** active
- **First observed:** 2026-08-21
- **Evidence:** PR #46; `src/mdx/i_didnt_put_my_screen_up.mdx`
- **Applies to:** drafting, reflective essays, work writing

## Context

The first draft of *I Didn't Put My Screen Up* followed a familiar generated arc: scene, abstraction, AI complication, universal leadership lesson and polished question ending. Phil rejected that structure.

The accepted piece stayed with the specific behaviour he had noticed in himself: deliberately not putting his laptop on the monitor at an internal hackathon. It kept the humour of the HDMI-cable detail, allowed AI to complicate the observation, and returned to the experience rather than converting it into a named leadership model.

## Learning

When the source is a small observed behaviour, let it remain a small observed behaviour for as long as possible.

Do not assume that a useful post needs to turn the moment into a framework, principle, checklist or universal leadership claim. Follow the implications that are genuinely present, but preserve the scale of the evidence.

## Why this matters

Phil is comfortable drawing wider implications from ordinary situations, but he is suspicious of making a moment look more important or more complete than it was. Over-structuring removes some of the credibility and humour that come from admitting the observation was simply interesting in its own right.

## When to apply

Retrieve this lesson for personal work reflections, leadership observations, hackathons, meetings, family moments and any draft whose source material is one concrete incident.

## Do not overgeneralise

This does not ban frameworks. A framework is appropriate when Phil has actually developed one or the evidence requires structured explanation. The warning is against manufacturing one because the draft otherwise feels too small.
