# Reading history is provenance, not decoration

- **Status:** active
- **First observed:** 2026-08-11
- **Evidence:** PR #42; `src/mdx/what_im_reading_and_what_im_noticing.mdx`
- **Applies to:** drafting, provenance, reading notes

## Context

The original reading-note draft flattened several books into a present-tense reading pile. Phil's review restored the fact that some were rereads, including the earlier route through *Zeitgeist*, Fuller and systems thinking, and corrected the strength of his reaction to *The Sovereign Individual*.

Those changes were not ornamental biography. They changed what the selection of books meant and how confidently the article could describe Phil's relationship with them.

## Learning

When writing from books, articles or saved sources, preserve the author's actual relationship with the material: first encounter, reread, long-standing influence, half-remembered connection, recent discovery or something saved without a settled view.

Do not turn a source list into a cleaner narrative by silently making all sources equally new, equally important or equally understood.

## Why this matters

Phil's voice often comes from the path by which an idea became interesting, not merely from the idea itself. Removing that history can make a technically accurate reading note feel generic and can accidentally invent a stronger or simpler relationship with a source than the evidence supports.

## When to apply

Retrieve this lesson for reading notes, Commonplace-derived essays and drafts that connect several books, articles or older ideas.

## Do not overgeneralise

Not every source needs a memoir attached to it. Include reading history only when it is known and materially changes the interpretation. Never invent a prior influence because it would make the essay cohere better.
