# Author-supplied language outranks a smoother synonym

- **Status:** active
- **First observed:** 2026-09-04
- **Evidence:** PR #54; `src/mdx/the_things_that_never_became_data.mdx`; PR #67 / Issue #66; `src/mdx/starting_something_new_while_returning_to_heritage.mdx`
- **Applies to:** drafting, editing, vocabulary, endings

## Context

During the final passes on *The Things That Never Became Data*, Phil rejected `ephemeral` as a word he would not naturally use. He also supplied the language that ultimately carried the ending: the reflection on the last in-person conversation with his Grandad, the difficulty of describing the emotion of the moment, “A real human moment” and “Put that in your context window.”

The accepted draft became stronger by moving toward Phil's actual wording rather than toward a more literary approximation of it.

In the final pass on the Malta heritage reflection (PR #67), Phil changed the smooth formulation that Linda "effectively made me have a drink with her" into two sentences: "She MADE me have a drink with her." He also changed "several hours" to "a few hours". Both corrections matter: the first restores the way he actually tells the story and its playful emphasis; the second keeps a casual account at the specificity he intended rather than an editor's more formal measure of duration.

## Learning

When Phil supplies a phrase, sentence, joke, analogy or correction that expresses the thought naturally, treat it as stronger voice evidence than an AI-generated synonym that sounds more polished.

Do not upgrade ordinary language merely to make the prose sound writerly. If a generated word is semantically correct but outside Phil's normal vocabulary, prefer the plainer supported alternative.

This applies beyond individual synonyms. Spoken emphasis, sentence breaks and ordinary approximations of time or scale can carry the author's register. A smoother sentence may lose the character of the incident; a seemingly harmless change from "a few" to "several" may subtly overstate or formalise the account.

The same rule applies to endings: author-supplied language with real provenance should not be replaced by a cleaner summary simply because the summary is rhetorically balanced.

## Why this matters

Voice is not just sentence shape. Vocabulary carries social register, confidence and personality. Replacing the author's own phrase with a more impressive equivalent can preserve meaning while losing authorship.

The strongest evidence for how Phil would say something is often Phil actually saying it.

## When to apply

Retrieve this lesson during Phil passes, rewrites from rough notes or dictation, and any draft where the source material contains distinctive author wording.

## Do not overgeneralise

Do not turn this specific use of capitals into a general instruction to capitalise words for emphasis, or infer that short sentences are always better. Preserve emphasis where Phil explicitly chooses it and it serves the particular moment.

Do not preserve every rough phrase literally. Grammar, ambiguity and readability still matter. The principle is that editing should clarify Phil's language, not silently substitute a more literary persona for it.
