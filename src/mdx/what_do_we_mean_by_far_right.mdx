Some time ago, I read [*The Tyranny of Words* by Stuart Chase](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL6375601M/The_tyranny_of_words).

Published in 1938, the book explored the relationship between language, thought and the world that language claims to describe. The challenge I retained from it was deceptively simple:

**What do you mean?**

Not what emotional response does the word produce?

Not which political side normally uses it?

Not what sort of person does it allow us to imagine?

What does the word actually refer to?

I have found myself returning to that question whenever I hear the term **far right**.

## Words are not the things they describe

We could not think or communicate without categories.

It would be impossible to describe every person, object or political belief from first principles each time it appeared. Words allow us to recognise patterns, condense information and share ideas.

But the word is not the thing.

A label is useful only while it remains connected closely enough to the reality beneath it.

This is relatively easy to see with physical objects. We can point to a tree, a table or a house. There may be disagreement around the edges, but most people understand what the word is trying to identify.

Abstract political terms are different.

We cannot point to democracy in the same way. We can point towards elections, laws, institutions, rights and peaceful transfers of power, but people may still carry different ideas about which of those features matter most.

The further a word moves from something directly observable, the more room it has to collect additional meanings.

Eventually, people may use the same word while referring to substantially different things.

They believe they are arguing about reality.

Often, they are arguing about the contents of the word.

That was the insight from Chase that stayed with me.

## Why 1938 matters

The publication date is difficult to ignore.

Chase was writing at a time when Europe was experiencing the growth of fascist and authoritarian government. Nazi propaganda did not merely criticise particular people or ideas. It helped [define varied groups as collective enemies and outsiders](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/defining-the-enemy), using repeated categories to make their exclusion appear legitimate.

It would be lazy to suggest that every modern political disagreement is a repetition of 1930s Europe.

Britain today is not Nazi Germany. Calling every objectionable political development “fascism” would itself demonstrate the problem Chase was examining.

The historical connection is more modest.

Chase was writing about unstable meaning while the world around him was demonstrating that political language could have consequences far beyond an argument about vocabulary.

Words could classify people.

Categories could replace individuals.

The label could determine what others believed about somebody before their conduct or character had been examined.

Eventually, the word did not merely describe the enemy.

It helped create the social idea of one.

The lesson is not that we should avoid naming dangerous ideologies. Fascism was real. Racial persecution was real. Political violence was real.

The lesson is that politically powerful words must remain attached to identifiable beliefs, behaviour and consequences.

## A word that keeps expanding

I returned to these questions after the murders in Southport and the disorder that followed.

There was genuine violence. Police officers and mosques were attacked. Racist rhetoric and Nazi salutes were visible. On 1 August 2024, the Prime Minister described the immediate challenge as being driven by [“far-right hatred”](https://www.gov.uk/government/speeches/prime-minister-keir-starmers-statement-in-downing-street-1-august). His statement was directed at violent criminality and those encouraging it.

I understood what he was condemning.

I condemned it too.

But I became concerned about what happened to the words once they entered the wider political conversation.

The term seemed to travel beyond the people throwing bricks, setting fires or targeting communities.

It began to appear around people concerned about immigration, people who distrusted government, people questioning institutional failure, people displaying national flags and people expressing unfashionable opinions in language that would never survive a professional communications review.

Some of those people may hold extreme views.

Some may be racist.

Some may be using ordinary concerns as respectable cover for something uglier.

But are all of them members of the same political tradition?

If the term applies equally to violent racial nationalists, authoritarian movements, dissatisfied voters and somebody who thinks immigration is too high, what information does it still provide?

It may tell us that the speaker disapproves.

It tells us less clearly what is actually there.

## Locating the meaning

So what do we mean by *far right*?

Does it describe belief in racial hierarchy?

An ethnically exclusive definition of citizenship?

The rejection of democratic government?

A desire for authoritarian rule?

The acceptance of intimidation or political violence?

Those are serious and recognisable positions. A term that helps us identify them has value.

But does it also include concern about the level of immigration?

Anxiety about integration?

Distrust of public institutions?

Attachment to national identity?

Attendance at a peaceful protest?

Voting for a party outside the established political centre?

Speaking badly about any of the above?

These things are not necessarily unrelated.

Extremist movements often attach themselves to legitimate grievances and familiar symbols. A concern can be exploited. A peaceful crowd can contain people with more sinister intentions. Someone may begin with distrust and travel towards an ideology that offers simple enemies and absolute explanations.

But connection is not equivalence.

Concern about immigration is not automatically belief in racial supremacy.

Distrust of government is not necessarily a desire to abolish democracy.

Patriotism is not fascism.

Clumsy language is not proof of hatred.

If we lose those distinctions, we may gain moral certainty while losing descriptive accuracy.

## The people beneath the category

Part of my concern comes from knowing people who could now find themselves placed beneath this label.

I went to the local comprehensive school. My friends include people from different backgrounds, faiths, classes and political traditions. My maternal family includes migrants. I have also spent most of my working life in public service, where I have seen both the seriousness of government responsibility and the subtle ways a boundary forms around what is considered acceptable opinion.

None of this makes me immune from prejudice or uniquely qualified to interpret society.

It does make the questions personal.

My own position is fairly straightforward.

I couldn’t give a toss where somebody comes from, what colour their skin is, what they believe or who they sleep with. If they live peacefully and do not wish or inflict harm on others, then I’m good.

I think that is true of most people I know.

They are not uniformly wise or tolerant. Neither am I.

They can be wrong. They can repeat claims without checking them. They can express a legitimate concern in a way that is careless or offensive. Knowing somebody to be kind in ordinary life does not make every political opinion they hold harmless.

But knowing the person does make it harder to accept that one concern or one badly chosen sentence reveals their entire ideology.

People are inconsistent.

They can hold generous and ungenerous ideas at the same time. They can be anxious without being hateful. They can identify a real problem and misunderstand its cause. They can distrust an institution for poor reasons—or because that institution has genuinely failed them.

A label may conceal all of that.

## When the word completes the argument

Political labels offer efficiency.

Once somebody has been called far right, the difficult work can appear complete.

We no longer need to ask what they actually believe.

We do not need to distinguish between legal migration, irregular migration, asylum, integration, population growth or pressure on services.

We do not need to ask whether their concern is practical, cultural, economic, prejudiced or some unstable mixture of several things.

The category has already supplied the explanation.

It can become both the accusation and the evidence.

The person is far right because they hold an unacceptable opinion. The opinion is unacceptable because it belongs to somebody described as far right.

The reasoning closes in upon itself.

Even asking for a definition can become suspicious.

Why are you so concerned about defending the far right?

But asking what a word means is not necessarily a defence of the people to whom it is applied.

It may be an attempt to preserve the word’s ability to identify something important.

## The difficulty of naming danger

We cannot solve careless political language by refusing to use political labels at all.

The far right is not imaginary. Neither are fascism, racial nationalism, authoritarianism or political violence.

There are beliefs and movements that reject equal citizenship, organise society around ethnicity or regard intimidation and force as legitimate political tools.

We need language capable of identifying them.

That is precisely why the meaning of the words matters.

If *far right* describes both a movement seeking racial supremacy and a person questioning the current level of immigration, the term is being asked to contain substantially different things.

Those positions may sometimes meet. Extremists may deliberately operate within a larger population of people carrying more ordinary concerns.

But proximity is not equivalence.

When language for genuine extremism becomes routine vocabulary for political disagreement, people who are not extremists begin to hear it merely as an insult.

Actual extremists may then gain room to hide within the much larger population carrying the same label.

Careless language creates two risks at once.

It may condemn people inaccurately.

And it may make the real danger harder to recognise.

## Do we learn from history?

We often say that we should learn from history.

Usually, we mean remembering the outcomes.

We remember wars, dictatorships, persecution and the moments when political violence became impossible to deny.

We are perhaps less successful at recognising the habits of thought that preceded them.

The simplification of complicated populations.

The use of a category as though it explains every individual within it.

The repetition of words whose emotional force has become stronger than their definition.

The assumption that questioning the label means sympathising with the worst people placed beneath it.

The decision that some citizens’ fears deserve compassion while others merely prove their moral deficiency.

History does not have to repeat precisely for its warnings to remain useful.

Its value may lie in helping us notice familiar intellectual habits before the consequences become familiar too.

That is what a book published in 1938 still offers.

Not a direct comparison between then and now.

A discipline.

Slow down.

Define the term.

Look beneath it.

Ask whether the word is helping us observe reality or merely organising our emotional response to it.

## What is actually there?

I am concerned about the increasing use of the term *far right*.

Not because I believe the far right is imaginary.

Not because racism, authoritarianism or political violence should be described more gently.

And not because everyone placed within the category is simply misunderstood.

I am concerned because the word sometimes appears to be expanding faster than its meaning can support.

Perhaps a substantial proportion of British society really has adopted an extremist political tradition.

That would be a profoundly serious development.

But before accepting something so bleak, we should ask which beliefs are present, which actions justify the description and whether we are using one term for several very different things.

The challenge I retained from Stuart Chase was not a demand for neutrality.

It was a demand for clarity.

**What do you mean?**

Behind that is an equally important question:

**What is actually there?**

Some words help us recognise danger.

Others gradually become a substitute for looking.
