I was doing rehabilitation after an injury and feeling rather sorry for myself.

Nothing especially dramatic had happened. There had been no spectacular accident and no heroic effort cut short. Just the familiar frustration of a body that would not do what I thought it ought to do, followed by a set of exercises that were useful, repetitive and considerably less interesting than the activities they were supposed to help me return to.

I understood the process.

Rest where necessary. Strengthen what was weak. Repeat the movements. Allow the body time to adapt.

Knowing that did not stop me resenting it.

Around the same time, my brother-in-law posted about an ultramarathon he had attempted. He had covered **66.7 miles** before a problem with his knee brought the effort to an end.

It was an incredible effort, although not entirely surprising. He is one of those people who prepares meticulously for the things he undertakes. He studies the problem, makes the plan, acquires the right equipment and does the work. He does not tend to arrive at difficult things hoping that enthusiasm will carry him through.

What caught my attention was his own reflection on the attempt.

Despite everything he had done, he did not believe he had conditioned himself for running beyond marathon distance.

At first, that sounded almost absurd.

Somebody who has run 66.7 miles is plainly conditioned in a way that most of us are not. The distance itself was evidence of preparation, resilience and an unusual ability to keep moving when stopping must have become increasingly attractive.

But his point was more precise.

He was not saying that he was unfit.

He was not saying that he lacked determination.

He was saying that the demands beyond a particular distance were different from those for which his body had been prepared.

That distinction stayed with me.

## The edge of preparation

We often talk about conditioning as though it were a general quality.

Somebody is fit or unfit. Resilient or fragile. Disciplined or lazy. Ready or unready.

The reality is more specific.

A person can be strong but not conditioned for prolonged endurance. They can be comfortable running a marathon and encounter a different physical problem after fifty miles. They can possess enormous determination while lacking the adaptation required by a particular movement, terrain, pace or duration.

Capability has conditions attached to it.

This is obvious in sport because the body is an unsentimental judge. It does not care how convincing the plan looked, how motivated we felt when we wrote it or how much we want the outcome.

At some point, the preparation either exists in muscle, tendon, movement, pacing and recovery—or it does not.

Character may carry us to the edge of our preparation.

It does not make the edge disappear.

There is also no shame in finding it.

Preparation is not a promise of complete control. It increases what we are capable of doing. It cannot guarantee that the body will behave as predicted, that injury will not occur or that reality will remain within the range we modelled beforehand.

Sometimes stopping is not evidence of insufficient toughness.

Sometimes it is the most disciplined decision left.

## Knowing was not my problem

My own situation was much less impressive.

I was not discovering the limits of my body after more than sixty miles. I was trying to recover properly from an injury and return to the things I enjoyed doing.

The principles were not difficult to understand.

I knew why the rehabilitation exercises mattered. I knew that avoiding them would probably delay recovery. I knew that progress would come through repetition rather than one unusually committed afternoon.

Information was not the problem.

I had been shown what to do. I could read more about it, ask questions and find detailed explanations of which muscles were supposed to be doing what.

None of that removed the central requirement.

I still had to do the exercises.

This was irritating because understanding something creates its own illusion of progress. Once we can explain the problem, describe the solution and perhaps even advise somebody else about it, part of us feels as though the work is already under way.

Often it is not.

I had access to the answer.

What I did not have was a way of outsourcing the adaptation.

## Human conditioning in the AI age

The phrase that began forming in my mind was **human conditioning for performance in the AI age**.

That is an unnecessarily grand way of describing a question that began with a Facebook post and me sulking about rehabilitation, but the question seemed worth holding on to.

What does human capability mean when increasingly sophisticated advice, analysis and output can be produced almost instantly?

Artificial intelligence can already help create a training plan, explain a rehabilitation exercise, prepare an interview presentation, structure a strategy or identify the steps required to deliver a complicated project.

That is useful.

It may also be profoundly democratising.

Good guidance has often depended on knowing the right person, belonging to the right organisation or being able to pay for somebody else's time and expertise. AI is reducing some of those access barriers. People who are committed enough to learn can now begin things that might once have appeared beyond their reach.

But easier access to instruction creates a new risk.

We may begin to mistake access to capability for capability itself.

A convincing plan can be produced before the person receiving it has developed any ability to follow it.

A polished strategy can be written before an organisation has learned how to make the decisions it requires.

A clear explanation can arrive without anybody having experienced the difficulty it describes.

The output may be excellent.

The recipient may remain unchanged.

## The plan is not the adaptation

This is not an argument that artificial intelligence makes people lazy.

People found ways to avoid difficult work long before computers could write competent prose.

Nor is it an argument that AI cannot help us develop real capability. It can provide feedback, adapt a programme, identify patterns and make knowledge more accessible. Used well, it may help people train more intelligently and avoid mistakes that once had to be learned through injury or expensive failure.

But the plan is not the adaptation.

A machine can describe progressive overload. It cannot strengthen my hip on my behalf.

It can generate a thoughtful account of resilience. It cannot expose me gradually to discomfort until I respond differently to it.

It can help prepare for a difficult conversation. It cannot build trust with the person sitting on the other side of the table.

It can produce the words through which change is described.

It cannot guarantee that change has entered the habits of the people expected to deliver it.

That gap may become more important as the quality of the output improves.

Poor documents are relatively easy to distrust. They look unfinished. The contradictions are visible. The lack of thought announces itself.

A beautifully produced answer is more persuasive.

It can create the impression that the difficult part has already happened.

## Organisations have conditioning too

The same distinction appears inside organisations.

An organisation can have an ambitious strategy, modern technology, governance diagrams and a programme full of carefully named workstreams.

It may still be unconditioned for the change it has promised.

When pressure arrives, organisations tend to fall back upon their established habits.

Can decisions actually be made?

Does ownership remain clear when work crosses departmental boundaries?

Do people share information when doing so feels inconvenient or risky?

Are standards followed only while somebody is checking?

Can leaders tolerate unwelcome evidence?

Will teams cooperate when resources become tight and institutional loyalties are tested?

These are not questions answered by the strategy itself.

They are answered by what the organisation has repeatedly practised.

Some organisations spend years training the muscles that produce plans, presentations and approval papers while neglecting those required to carry change under load.

They have, in effect, been skipping leg day.

Then a major transformation arrives, or a crisis, or a reorganisation, and everybody is surprised that the institution cannot perform at the distance described in its own documents.

The ambition may be sincere.

The route may be sensible.

The organisation may still not be conditioned for it.

## Discomfort is not the point

There is a temptation in writing about endurance to make pain sound morally cleansing.

It is easy to turn suffering into proof of character and stopping into evidence of weakness. There is some truth in the importance of deliberate discomfort. Capability rarely develops without resistance.

But pain is not automatically progress.

Injury is not a moral achievement.

Exhaustion does not make every decision noble.

There is a difference between exposing ourselves to enough difficulty to adapt and treating damage as evidence that the exercise worked.

My brother-in-law's preparation carried him 66.7 miles. That preparation mattered. It made an extraordinary effort possible.

It did not make his body unlimited.

Recognising that boundary does not diminish the achievement. It makes the achievement more real.

## The smaller distance

I found myself turning his experience into a large intellectual question while my own version of conditioning remained stubbornly ordinary.

Do the exercises.

Do them again.

Accept that the body I remembered was not automatically the body available to me now.

Stop treating rehabilitation as an interruption to the real activity and recognise that, for the moment, rehabilitation was the activity.

There was no heroic photograph attached to that.

No impressive distance.

No public test of endurance.

Just the slow conversion of intention into capability.

Perhaps that is why the idea stayed with me.

AI is likely to make it easier for us to describe the person, organisation or society we would like to become. It will give us better plans, clearer explanations and increasingly convincing versions of work that once required considerable expertise to produce.

That is a genuine opportunity.

But there will still be a distance between possessing the answer and becoming capable of living it.

The answers may become almost effortless.

The conditioning probably will not.
