There's a particular kind of satisfaction in solving an urgent problem. Something breaks, people rally round, a workaround is found and normal service resumes. Everyone can get on with their day, and the people who stepped in receive a well-deserved pat on the back. I've done plenty of this myself, and I know how useful it can be. What I question is whether we've made progress beyond dealing with the immediate problem.

Sometimes a temporary fix is exactly the right decision. But when the same underlying problem returns, perhaps in a slightly different form, we tend to repeat the exercise. Someone sorts it out, we're grateful, and the more difficult work of understanding why it keeps happening is put off again.

Over time the workaround becomes part of the process. Other things depend on it. People become experts in keeping it alive, which makes changing it seem even more difficult. The problem we didn't have time to solve properly has grown into one that will take considerably more time to put right, and everyone remains busy keeping it working.

I've [written before about being busy](/were_all_busy_thats_why_the_strategy_matters/) and why having a strategy should help us make choices about where our effort goes. I've also explored [the space between incentives](/the_space_between_incentives/): the possibility that individually sensible behaviour can preserve something that isn't working particularly well for the organisation as a whole. There is an obvious incentive to get the immediate problem off your desk, especially when somebody is waiting for it to be fixed. The reward for addressing the root cause is less immediate, and often much less visible.

My [Data Werewolf](/the_data_werewolf/) was an attempt to describe the change that can take place when evidence starts to challenge an accepted account of how things are working. A question about a number, a process or a recurring failure can quickly become a discussion about somebody's competence or an earlier decision. The person asking the question starts to look like the problem.

I don't think that reaction is always deliberate. People care about their work, and it's uncomfortable to have something you've done questioned. Sometimes the resistance is about motivation; sometimes it may be a lack of confidence or capability. I suspect it's often a bit of both. I'm not immune to feeling defensive either, particularly when I've put a lot into something.

But we have to be able to separate legitimate challenge from personal criticism. Equally, we need to recognise when disagreement has crossed into genuinely poor behaviour, rather than treating every objection as another valid point of view. That requires leadership prepared to make the distinction and deal with it, rather than simply hoping everyone will get along.

My son was the reason I got involved in youth football. I started coaching his team at under-sevens and stayed with it through to under-sixteens. As is often the case when I become interested in something, I went all in. I took my Level 1 coaching qualification and eventually worked my way through to the UEFA B Licence. I wanted to understand the game, but also how you help a group of young people learn, improve and become a team.

We were hardly world-beaters at the beginning. In our first season we lost every game. The team evolved over the years, as they do, but much of the same core stayed together. By 2018, playing as St Ives Rangers Under 11 White Sox, we'd won all 22 of our league and cup matches, finishing with a 4–1 cup final victory. The [Hunts Post even called us a band of footballing invincibles](https://www.huntspost.co.uk/sport/22974832.colts-football-cup-final-triumph-caps-unbeaten-season-super-st-ives-rangers-lads/). I coached alongside Matt Vickery that season, and seeing the boys rewarded for all that work was a lovely moment for everyone involved.

From early on, there were some simple things we insisted on. Players shook hands with coaches and teammates before and after sessions and matches. Being on time, bringing the right kit and treating people properly were expected. Those were our non-negotiables. They weren't complicated, and I'm certainly not claiming that shaking hands was the secret to winning football matches, but they gave us a consistent way of doing things.

Within that, I wanted the boys to think for themselves. We encouraged them to try things, make mistakes and work out what to do when something wasn't going to plan. We didn't always have the best individual players on paper, but we developed a team that could achieve more together than the individuals might have been expected to achieve separately.

My current studies in creativity and innovation have made me think again about those coaching years, particularly the part constraints can play in creative thinking. We were quite firm about some things and gave the boys plenty of freedom in others. If we'd tried to control every decision on the pitch or prevent every mistake, I'm not sure how they'd have learned to make those decisions themselves.

The direction remained broadly consistent as the team developed, but our approach changed when it needed to. We got to know the boys as individuals, and what worked with one wasn't necessarily right for another. Sometimes a jolt was needed; sometimes an arm round the shoulder. Knowing when to challenge someone, how to go about it and when to give them space was part of the coaching. I think we were better at that than some, although we didn't always get it right. When I got things wrong, I took responsibility and we worked out how to put them right. The players did the hard work, and they were the ones who won the matches. My job was to give them the platform, challenge them, encourage them and, I hoped, help them grow as people as well as footballers.

I'm wary of taking the football comparison too far. Professional organisations have different pressures and competing responsibilities, but I do find the contrast striking. In coaching, I saw how consistency, clear expectations and a shared purpose could help people take more responsibility for themselves and one another. It also took time to build the trust that made an honest challenge possible. I haven't experienced that same kind of consistent leadership and support at work for a long time. There are probably several reasons for that, but I do think knowing your team, understanding how different people respond and having the skill to make the right intervention are too easily overlooked. Meanwhile, I sometimes see very capable people stuck in arrangements that require them to keep fixing the same things, while the decisions needed to change those arrangements remain unresolved.

A strategy is only useful if we're prepared to use it when the difficult requests arrive. That includes challenging work that takes us away from the direction we've agreed, even if the request comes from someone senior. It means being clear about what can wait, what needs changing and who is responsible for deciding. It also means giving people the confidence to surface problems without treating them as disloyal, while being prepared to address behaviour that genuinely undermines the team.

I know that's easier to write than to do. There will always be urgent problems, reasonable compromises and occasions when we simply don't have the resources to do everything properly. What I struggle with is the absence of a conscious decision. If we're choosing a temporary fix, let's understand what we're accepting, who's accountable for it and when we're going to do the real work. Otherwise the same people will be back doing another good job of fixing it.

Last weekend I bumped into some of the lads I used to coach. They're young men now, but we still stop and talk about those heady days when we were champions. There's a mutual respect that has lasted long after we stopped playing football together.

You can't buy that.
