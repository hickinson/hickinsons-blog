There's a particular kind of satisfaction in solving an urgent problem. Something breaks, people rally round, a workaround is found and normal service resumes. Everyone can get on with their day, and the people who stepped in receive a well-deserved pat on the back.

I've been wondering how often we mistake that for progress.

I'm not knocking the people doing the fixing. I've done plenty of it myself, and there's a lot to be said for getting stuck in and making something work. Sometimes a temporary fix is exactly the right decision. But when the same underlying problem returns, perhaps in a slightly different form, we tend to repeat the exercise. Someone sorts it out, we're grateful, and the more difficult work of understanding why it keeps happening is put off again.

Over time the workaround becomes part of the process. Other things depend on it. People become experts in keeping it alive, which makes changing it seem even more difficult. The problem we didn't have time to solve properly has grown into one that will take considerably more time to put right.

We're still busy, of course. That's part of what troubles me.

I've [written before about being busy](/were_all_busy_thats_why_the_strategy_matters/) and why having a strategy should help us make choices about where our effort goes. I've also explored [the space between incentives](/the_space_between_incentives/): the possibility that individually sensible behaviour can preserve something that isn't working particularly well for the organisation as a whole. There is an obvious incentive to get the immediate problem off your desk, especially when somebody is waiting for it to be fixed. The reward for addressing the root cause is less immediate, and often much less visible.

Then there's the question of what happens when somebody does try to get to the bottom of it.

My [Data Werewolf](/the_data_werewolf/) was an attempt to describe the change that can take place when evidence starts to challenge an accepted account of how things are working. A question about a number, a process or a recurring failure can quickly become a discussion about somebody's competence or an earlier decision. The person asking the question starts to look like the problem.

I don't think that reaction is always deliberate. People care about their work, and it's uncomfortable to have something you've done questioned. Sometimes the resistance is about motivation; sometimes it may be a lack of confidence or capability. I suspect it's often a bit of both. I'm not immune to feeling defensive either, particularly when I've put a lot into something.

But we have to be able to separate legitimate challenge from personal criticism. Equally, we need to recognise when disagreement has crossed into genuinely poor behaviour, rather than treating every objection as another valid point of view. That requires leadership prepared to make the distinction and deal with it, rather than simply hoping everyone will get along.

I've been thinking about all this in the context of something quite different: the years I spent coaching youth football.

My son was the reason I got involved. I started coaching his team at under-sevens and stayed with it through to under-sixteens. As is often the case when I become interested in something, I went all in. I took my Level 1 coaching qualification and eventually worked my way through to the UEFA B Licence. I wanted to understand the game, but also how you help a group of young people learn, improve and become a team.

We were hardly world-beaters at the beginning. In our first season we lost every game. The team evolved over the years, as they do, but much of the same core stayed together. By 2018, playing as St Ives Rangers Under 11 White Sox, we'd won all 22 of our league and cup matches, finishing with a 4–1 cup final victory. The [Hunts Post even called us a band of footballing invincibles](https://www.huntspost.co.uk/sport/22974832.colts-football-cup-final-triumph-caps-unbeaten-season-super-st-ives-rangers-lads/). I coached alongside Matt Vickery that season, and it was a lovely moment for everyone involved.

I'm proud of those results, but what interests me now is how we got there.

From early on, there were some simple things we insisted on. Players shook hands with coaches and teammates before and after sessions and matches. Being on time, bringing the right kit and treating people properly were expected. Those were our non-negotiables. They weren't complicated, and I'm certainly not claiming that shaking hands was the secret to winning football matches, but they gave us a consistent way of doing things.

Within that, I wanted the boys to think for themselves. We encouraged them to try things, make mistakes and work out what to do when something wasn't going to plan. We didn't always have the best individual players on paper, but we developed a team that could achieve more together than the individuals might have been expected to achieve separately.

There's a connection here with my current studies in creativity and innovation. We've been exploring how constraints can shape creative thinking, and I keep coming back to those coaching years. The boundaries were quite firm in some areas, but they gave us room to be much more open in others. We weren't trying to remove every mistake or control every decision on the pitch. How would the players ever learn to make those decisions themselves?

The direction remained broadly consistent as the team developed, but our approach changed when it needed to. When I got things wrong, I took responsibility and we worked out how to put them right. The players did the hard work, and they were the ones who won the matches. My job was to give them the platform, challenge them, encourage them and, I hoped, help them grow as people as well as footballers.

I don't pretend a youth football team is the same as a large professional organisation. The demands and competing responsibilities are different. But I do find the contrast striking. In coaching, I saw how consistency, clear expectations and a shared purpose could help people take more responsibility for themselves and one another. In organisations, I sometimes see very capable people stuck in arrangements that require them to keep fixing the same things, while the decisions needed to change those arrangements remain unresolved.

A strategy is only useful if we're prepared to use it when the difficult requests arrive. That includes challenging work that takes us away from the direction we've agreed, even if the request comes from someone senior. It means being clear about what can wait, what needs changing and who is responsible for deciding. It also means giving people the confidence to surface problems without treating them as disloyal, while being prepared to address behaviour that genuinely undermines the team.

I know that's easier to write than to do. There will always be urgent problems, reasonable compromises and occasions when we simply don't have the resources to do everything properly. What I struggle with is the absence of a conscious decision. If we're choosing a temporary fix, let's understand what we're accepting, who's accountable for it and when we're going to do the real work. Otherwise the same people will be back doing another good job of fixing it.

Last weekend I bumped into some of the lads I used to coach. They're young men now, but we still stop and talk about those days when we were champions. The respect we established when they were boys is still there, and so are the stories. My son and I have the trophies, of course, but those conversations are something else.

You can't buy that.
