This is one of those photographs that makes the story look much simpler than it was.

My son is smiling, holding the belts he had just won, with one of his teammates beside him. The contest is finished, the result is settled and there is something physical he can take home that says he was the champion that day.

As his dad, I was immensely proud of him. Not only because he had won, although pretending that the result did not matter would be dishonest. He had entered to win a title and came away with it. That mattered to him, so it mattered to me.

What made the moment more significant was everything that had happened before it. There had been other contests and other journeys home without the result he wanted. He already knew what it felt like to prepare properly, commit himself and still finish disappointed.

That is the part the photograph cannot show.

It cannot show the training after a defeat, when the excitement of the next event is still some distance away. It cannot show the conversations about what went wrong, or the temptation to explain away a result rather than learn from it. It cannot show the gradual process of going back, listening, improving and agreeing to place yourself in the same vulnerable position again.

Losing once removes some of the innocence from ambition. Before the disappointment, it is possible to believe that wanting something badly enough and working hard should be sufficient. Afterwards, you know better. You know that effort does not guarantee the result, that somebody else may be better on the day, and that preparation can still end in a long and quiet journey home.

Returning after that requires a different kind of confidence. Not the confidence that assumes you will win, but the confidence to continue when you know you might not.

I have spent much of my life around sport. I played football, coached it and watched my children develop their own relationships with competition. Sport has always been one of the ways our family understands effort, teamwork, disappointment and progress.

Being a parent within that is not always easy. When children are young, you become accustomed to solving problems for them. You collect them, comfort them, replace what has broken and try to place yourself between them and anything that might cause harm. As they get older, the problems become more complicated and your ability to intervene becomes less useful.

You can support them. You can listen, encourage and occasionally offer advice they did not ask for. You can create some of the conditions around them. But you cannot compete on their behalf.

You cannot carry their nerves, make their decisions or absorb their disappointment. Nor can you protect them from every setback without also protecting them from the experiences through which they learn who they are.

That helplessness is part of watching. You want the result for them, but you also know that the result has to belong to them.

I was proud of him before the title was won. Proud that he had gone back after previous disappointments and remained willing to learn. Proud that he had continued putting himself in situations where the outcome could not be controlled. Proud that he had not decided that losing meant the effort had been wasted.

The victory made those things visible, but it did not create them.

Most of the important work in sport happens without a photograph. Nobody takes a picture after an ordinary training session or after an athlete responds well to difficult feedback. There is no belt for choosing discipline on a day when motivation is absent. No crowd gathers around the moment someone notices a weakness and decides to work on it rather than hide it.

We tend to see the result because results are easy to recognise. A hand is raised, a score is recorded and a belt is placed around someone’s waist. The preparation is less dramatic. It consists mostly of repetition, small corrections and days when very little appears to have changed.

That is probably true of most meaningful achievement. We see the graduation ceremony, not all the evenings spent studying around work and family. We see the promotion, not the setbacks that forced somebody to reconsider how ready they really were. We see the finished thing rather than the period when success was uncertain and few people were paying attention.

The photograph also reminds me that an individual result is rarely entirely individual. He is standing beside somebody from his team, an arm around his shoulder. He entered the competition himself, but he did not arrive there alone.

Coaches, teammates, friends and family all occupy different places around an athlete. Some provide technical knowledge, some challenge excuses and some offer stability when confidence becomes less reliable. The title belongs to the person who competed for it, but the journey is shaped by a group.

There is another challenge after winning, of course. Success can become its own distraction. A title proves what happened on one particular day; it does not guarantee the next result or remove the need to keep learning. The healthiest response is probably to celebrate it properly without allowing it to settle every question about what comes next.

But that can wait.

Some moments should be allowed to remain moments rather than immediately being converted into another lesson or target.

He had worked for it. He had experienced disappointment and returned. When the opportunity arrived, he was ready enough to take it.

So we took the photograph.

The belts are the first thing you notice, because they are large, gold and designed to be noticed. They show what he won.

What they cannot show is why I was proud.
