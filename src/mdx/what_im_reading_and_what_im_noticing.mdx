I’m reading a book about superintelligence, and naturally the thing that has stayed with me so far is the owl.

Nick Bostrom opens *Superintelligence* with a fable about sparrows considering bringing an owl into their lives. An owl could do things they cannot. Most of the sparrows are enthusiastic. A smaller number wonder whether it might be sensible to work out how to control an owl before inviting one in.

It is a neat way into a much bigger problem.

It also helps that I have a weakness for owls. I’m a Wednesdayite, so they come with the territory, and some of my friends have occasionally described me as “Owly”, whatever that means. Put an owl at the beginning of a book about artificial intelligence and apparently you have my attention.

That is probably as good a place as any to start some reading notes.

I’ve been getting back into reading properly. Not reading because I ought to, or because a particular book is new, but following books that seem to connect with questions I’m already carrying around.

These are not reviews. I haven’t finished forming views on all of them, and that is partly the point. This is simply what is catching my attention while they are on the pile.

## Superintelligence — Nick Bostrom

*Superintelligence* is not a new book, which is partly what makes reading it now interesting.

The owl story lands differently when artificial intelligence is no longer something I encounter mainly through speculation about the future. I use it. I build things with it. I ask it questions, use it to interrogate ideas and increasingly see it appearing inside ordinary work.

That does not mean we have arrived at Bostrom’s superintelligence. We plainly haven’t. But it changes the emotional distance from the argument.

The sparrows are no longer debating whether owls might theoretically be useful. There are feathers around the place.

What I like about the metaphor is that it keeps the question simple. The interesting part is not just what a more capable intelligence can do. It is what happens to the relationship once something much more capable is inside the system.

I suspect that image will stay with me longer than many of the technical details.

## Critical Path — R. Buckminster Fuller

I’m not far enough into *Critical Path* to pretend I have a settled view of it.

What I am looking for as I read is the willingness to ask questions at a much larger scale than most of us normally do. I spend a lot of time thinking about systems, organisations and the constraints people inherit without necessarily choosing them. That makes me interested in writers who are prepared to ask whether the structure itself is part of the problem.

I am also wary of explanations that become too complete. The bigger the theory, the easier it is for awkward details to disappear inside it.

That tension is probably why the book is on the pile in the first place: I want the provocation without feeling obliged to accept the whole system that comes with it.

## The Sovereign Individual — James Dale Davidson and William Rees-Mogg

This is another older book I am reading partly because I am interested in what happens when predictions about technology are allowed to age.

The broad territory — technology, individuals, institutions, geography and power — is already enough to make it relevant to questions I find myself returning to.

What interests me most at this stage is the gap between what technology makes possible and what people and institutions actually do with that possibility.

Constraints can disappear technically while surviving culturally, legally or organisationally. We can work from almost anywhere and still build working life around offices. We can give people extraordinary access to information without producing shared understanding. We can automate work and then somehow create more of it.

I do not yet know where I will land on the book’s wider argument. For now, that gap between technological change and institutional change is the thing I am watching.

## The Zeitgeist Movement Defined

This one sits most obviously in the systems corner of the pile.

Again, I am less interested in agreeing with an entire worldview than in seeing what happens when familiar problems are framed as connected parts of a larger system.

That instinct appeals to me. So does the discomfort that comes with it.

Systems thinking can make complexity feel as though it might be understandable if only we draw the boundaries correctly, identify the incentives and map the relationships. Sometimes that is genuinely useful.

But people have an irritating habit of refusing to behave like components in a diagram.

Any explanation of society eventually has to survive contact with people carrying histories, loyalties, fears, ambitions, families, egos, beliefs and very different ideas about what a good life looks like.

That is the part I am curious about as I read.

The four books do not form a coherent reading programme, and I do not particularly want them to.

One is concerned with intelligence that could exceed our own. Another asks questions at the scale of humanity and its systems. Another considers technology, power and the individual. Another attempts to rethink some of the assumptions beneath the way society operates.

I don’t expect to agree with all of them.

In fact, I hope I don’t.

I’ve become less interested in reading books that simply give me better language for things I already believe. The more useful ones tend to leave something irritating behind: a question, an objection, an idea I can’t quite dismiss.

At the moment, one of those things happens to be an owl.

Which, admittedly, was always likely to work on me.
