A phrase popped into my head this week for no particular reason: 'Committed Curiosity'. I've been thinking about it since and wondering whether it describes something about the way I've lived much of my life.

I was asked to think about some of the things I've been a little obsessed with over the years. Where to start? Music, DJ'ing, music production, football, coaching, running, the gym, programming, computer science, data, reading, education and, more recently, AI. Looking back, my motivations for pursuing these things were often quite different, although the way I went about them was probably quite similar. I'm generally an all-in kind of person.

I started running because I was getting fat and unfit. There wasn't much more to it than that initially, but as I started to see improvements, I became increasingly interested in running itself. I enjoyed setting myself targets and breaking them, which inevitably led to more ambitious targets and a desire to understand how to achieve them. Getting fit was the original motivation, but getting better at running became a source of enjoyment in itself.

Football was different. I'd been obsessed with the game from a young age, both playing and watching, and by the time I got into coaching I thought I knew quite a lot about it. Coaching tested that knowledge and gave me a very different perspective on something I'd spent much of my life enjoying. Understanding something yourself and helping other people develop their own understanding of it are quite different things. Much of what I learnt coaching kids has since been useful to me in managing and leading adults.

My interest in programming, software development and data was largely driven by a desire to understand my craft. I wanted to be the best version of myself at work. Interestingly, I can draw quite a few parallels between developing software and producing electronic music, something that occupied a considerable amount of my time in my younger years. I enjoy working with components, understanding what they do individually and experimenting with the ways they can be put together to create something. There's a familiarity to that creative process, regardless of whether I'm working with sounds or software.

I've often described myself as someone who knows a little about a lot. Increasingly, I'm finding that much of the value in that comes from being able to integrate what I've learnt. Studying creativity and leadership as part of my Masters has prompted me to revisit experiences from entirely different periods of my life and, in some cases, understand them differently. I doubt I'd have got the same things out of the course had I studied it in my twenties.

Do I regret not pursuing education earlier in life? A little, perhaps. I became a father at 20 and my priorities changed quite considerably. I also spent much of my early career more interested in music, DJ'ing and putting on parties than I was in developing my career! Whilst my motivation for studying now is largely about becoming better at what I do, I'd be lying if I said the academic achievements themselves didn't mean something to me. They're things I never really imagined achieving back then.

I really enjoyed reading *The Squiggly Career* by Helen Tupper and Sarah Ellis and have often thought I could write a book called *The Squiggly Life*. Our careers are so intertwined with everything else that's going on in our lives that it seems quite difficult to consider them in isolation. I've always said I don't regret anything because I'm conscious of how blessed I am, and I'm not sure I'd want to straighten out too many of those squiggles in mine.

Being all-in has its downsides, of course. I can be incredibly self-critical and I suspect that my enjoyment of getting better at things sometimes gets in the way of appreciating how far I've come. There's a peculiar thing about setting yourself a target, achieving it and then almost immediately using that achievement as the new measure against which to judge yourself. I wonder how much of that is ambition and how much is habit.

What's particularly interesting to me is that I've never really had a plan for how all these things might complement one another. I wasn't thinking about organisational leadership when I started coaching football, nor did I anticipate discussing my experiences of producing electronic music in the context of studying creativity at university.

Perhaps that's what I mean by committed curiosity. There doesn't necessarily need to be a long-term purpose behind everything I'm interested in, but once something captures my interest, I tend to want to understand it properly. Looking back, I'm quite surprised by how much of what I've learnt doing things I simply enjoyed has found its way into other aspects of my life.

It makes me wonder what else I might find interesting over the next twenty years.
