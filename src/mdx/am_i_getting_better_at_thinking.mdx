When I first started running, I could barely manage a 5k in under 30 minutes. I went on to run a marathon in under four hours. One of the things I enjoyed about running was being able to see the progress I was making. I could run further, I could run faster and I had the data to demonstrate it.

I'm now a few weeks into my Master's and, given that much of what I'm studying is concerned with how we think, I've been wondering how I'll know if I'm getting any better at it. Right at this moment I'm quite tired and still getting to grips with being back studying, so the answer is that I don't really know. I'd hope so!

Interestingly, I do feel that my speaking and presentation skills have improved, which is a bit of a mystery given that we haven't been specifically practising either. I wonder whether studying alongside people for whom English isn't necessarily their first language is forcing me to slow down a little and be clearer in my explanations. It's interesting that one of the first improvements I've noticed might be in something I wasn't consciously working on.

A lack of measurement is a recurring frustration of mine professionally. I'm keen to back up claims with evidence rather than anecdote, so it's quite interesting to turn some of that scrutiny on myself. Of course, evidence doesn't always need to be numerical and I'm interested in developing a better understanding of qualitative measurement as I get further into my studies.

Achieving a first-class degree last time around was evidence of an achievement, although I couldn't tell you precisely how much more capable I'd become. I could feel it, and the achievement itself played a part in developing the belief that I could go on and do other things, including what I'm doing now.

I wonder whether some of my professional frustration stems from viewing career progression as a measure of my own development. The difficulty with that is that you can be developing your capability without necessarily progressing in your career. I want the opportunity to demonstrate what I'm capable of, but equally I want the opportunity to grow.

In a conversation with ChatGPT while developing this piece, it asked me what would give me confidence that I'm a more capable person today than I was ten years ago if I took away my qualifications, job title and other recognised achievements.

I found that quite a tough question. I'd probably look at my family, who are thriving, and reflect on the fact that, whether through work or coaching, I feel I've generally left things in a better state than when I found them. Although in answering the question I found myself wanting to acknowledge the part that my achievements have played in developing that capability, as well as the belief that people around me have had in me.

Perhaps some of the evidence of our own development exists in the capabilities of the people around us. I know how important other people's belief in me has been, and I like the thought that I might have played a part in somebody else's development in much the same way.

I read something by JA Westenberg earlier this week, [Your Second Brain Is Just an Expensive Hard Drive](https://www.joanwestenberg.com/p/your-second-brain-is-just-an-expensive), which challenged the value of building a second brain, suggesting that we're often just accumulating information rather than doing anything particularly useful with it. Given the amount of time I've invested in building a system around my studies, it was a challenge worth considering.

What I'd quite like to do towards the end of my Master's is go back through some of those early notes and reflections. I'd be interested in whether I still approach problems in the same way, whether I've changed my mind about things and whether I can make connections that I wouldn't have made before. It would be nice to have some evidence that I've become better at thinking.

It won't be quite as easy as looking at my Garmin.
