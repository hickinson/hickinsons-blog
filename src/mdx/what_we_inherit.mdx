It's been a funny old week. I've felt like I've been fighting battles, putting a lot of energy into things and not necessarily getting very far. One of those weeks where you can have plenty of conversations, make perfectly reasonable arguments and still finish wondering whether anything has actually moved forward.

Then I watched *Pressure*, a film about the days leading up to the D-Day landings in June 1944.

The film follows James Stagg, the meteorologist responsible for bringing together the weather forecasts that would help determine when the Allied invasion could take place. It's an extraordinary story, and perhaps unsurprisingly, given my interest in data, I found myself fascinated by that side of it. Weather observations, competing interpretations, uncertainty and people having to make decisions with consequences almost impossible to comprehend.

I spend a lot of my working life thinking about data, evidence and the decisions people make with it. Watching people argue over what the information was telling them, while the pressure to reach a decision continued to build, certainly resonated. There's something familiar about presenting evidence that isn't necessarily what people want to hear, although thankfully the consequences of my working week are rather less significant.

But with Remembrance Sunday approaching, the film also got me thinking about my own family and the people who went through things most of us will thankfully never experience.

I've spent quite a bit of time researching my family history, particularly those who served during the First World War. One of the people I've focussed on most is my great-grandad, [John (Jack) Cotterill Hickinson](https://hathersage-remembers.org.uk/2016/11/15/hickinson-john-jack-cotterill/).

John was born in Hathersage in 1898 and enlisted in February 1916. His battalion, the 9th Royal Scots, served with the 51st Highland Division, fighting in some of the major battles of the war. He was eventually badly wounded in both legs, spending time in hospital before returning closer to home to recover at Longshaw. He survived the war and lived until 1967.

Much of what I know about his service has come from piecing together historical records and reading the diaries of soldiers serving in his battalion. I've spent time following their movements, trying to establish where they were and what they were doing, including their time at the Somme. It's fascinating research, although there's only so much you can establish about an individual through the experiences recorded by others.

I have photographs of John too. One shows him standing in his Highland uniform, a young man with his life ahead of him. Another shows him recovering in a hospital bed at Longshaw. It's difficult to look at those photographs, knowing what happened between them, and not wonder what he must have experienced.

Something that increasingly gets to me is that my own sons are now older than John was when he went to war.

I look at them and try to imagine what it would have been like for somebody their age to find themselves in his position. I'm sure John had his own ambitions and ideas about what life might hold, just as my sons do today. Instead, he found himself fighting a war, experiencing things that are difficult enough to comprehend more than a century later.

And when I think about how badly he was wounded, I can't help wondering just how close we all came to never existing.

Had John not survived, my grandad might never have been born. Nor my dad, nor me, nor my children. Four generations of our family, all depending on a young man's survival in circumstances I can scarcely imagine. When you start thinking about history in those terms, it becomes rather more than a collection of dates and events.

I've shared much of what I've discovered about John with my children, often when we've been talking about facing difficult things. I've developed a few expressions over the years, usually when somebody is struggling with something or feeling like giving up.

"We're in the trenches here, dig deep," is one of them.

I've also been known to say, "John didn't survive the Somme for us to be moaning about this."

They're fairly blunt expressions, and probably a bit unfair on whatever happens to be bothering us at the time, but the sentiment is genuine. I use them to remind myself as much as anyone else that sometimes you have to dig a little deeper and keep going. Life can be difficult, frustrating and occasionally feel rather unfair, but a bit of perspective rarely does any harm.

I think my understanding of sacrifice has changed as I've got older. When you're young, the world wars are largely historical events. You learn about the battles, the dates, the casualties and their significance, but I don't think I appreciated what those losses really meant. Perhaps it's having children, or simply accumulating a bit more life experience, that changes how you see things.

We rightly remember those who gave their lives, but I've also come to think more about those who survived. John came home, thankfully, although with serious injuries. Many others returned with wounds and experiences that would stay with them for the rest of their lives. Surviving the war didn't necessarily mean escaping its consequences.

Then there are those who never returned, something that affected my own family in the Second World War. Both of my grandmothers lost brothers, young men who were only in their twenties. Two families having to come to terms with losing somebody who should have had so much of life ahead of them.

I find myself thinking about the futures those men never had, just as I think about the generations that followed John because he survived. There must be countless families with similar stories, and many more whose family histories took entirely different paths because somebody never came home.

I often wonder what those men believed they were fighting for. Their country, their families, their friends or perhaps simply the person alongside them. I'm sure there were all sorts of reasons, and many had little choice in the matter. Whatever their circumstances, the consequences of what they endured have carried forward through generations, shaping the lives of people they would never know.

Coming back to my own week, the things that have frustrated me are still there. I'm sure I'll return to some of those battles next week, hopefully with a bit more success. But I've also got the opportunity to keep trying, to challenge things, change my mind, make mistakes and have another go. That's something worth remembering when I'm inclined to feel sorry for myself.

We say *lest we forget* every November. I've always understood the importance of remembrance, but the older I get, the more it seems to settle with me. Those young men gave up so much, and each generation since has inherited something from what they endured. We owe them an enormous amount, perhaps more than we can ever properly understand.

John has been gone since 1967, but more than a century after he went to war, I'm still learning about him, thinking about what he went through and telling my children his story.

And I find myself increasingly grateful that he made it home.
