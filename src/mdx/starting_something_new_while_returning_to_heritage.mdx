I've come to Malta to begin a Master's in Creativity and Innovation at the University of Malta's Edward de Bono Institute. I've just turned 46, and although I've spent a good part of my working life learning new things, starting another university course at this stage feels like quite an adventure.

There's another reason being here feels significant. I'm part Yorkshire and part Maltese, and although I've visited Malta before, this time I'm here for something other than a holiday. I've got lectures to attend, people to meet and a chance to spend a little more time getting to know a place I've always felt a connection to.

Malta is strangely familiar, despite being so different from England. There are the obvious connections through language and history, and plenty of reminders of Britain dotted around the island. On Sunday afternoon, I found myself in a pub watching Premier League football, which isn't an especially unusual thing for me to be doing.

What caught my attention, though, was a table of Maltese ladies of a certain age sitting nearby. They were chatting away, loudly and expressively, and something about their mannerisms immediately reminded me of my Nanna, who was from Gżira and is sadly no longer with us.

It wasn't anything particular they were saying. It was their tone, their expressions and the way they communicated with their whole bodies, with plenty of arm-waving thrown in for good measure. I recognised something of my Nanna in them, which was quite a strange experience given that I'd never met any of these women.

I've noticed that the Maltese can be wonderfully direct. They're expressive, often loud, and queuing doesn't always seem to be something they're especially concerned with. There's plenty of gesticulating too, something I've been accused of doing rather enthusiastically myself. Perhaps there's more Maltese in me than I realise.

It's difficult to explain the connection I feel to the place. I don't speak Maltese, and there are plenty of things about everyday life that are different from what I'm used to at home. Yet there's something about the people and the way they interact that feels familiar.

## The people you meet

Some of the most enjoyable moments of the trip so far have come from conversations I wasn't expecting to have.

On the flight over, I sat next to Linda, a lovely lady of traveller heritage who was well into her eighties and effectively made me have a drink with her. We ended up talking for almost the entire journey, and I thoroughly enjoyed her company.

Linda had plenty of stories to tell, some deeply moving and others rather funny. She spoke about losing her daughter, which was heartbreaking to hear, but also recounted an occasion involving rescuing drowning horses and somehow passing fleas on to the horses belonging to her rather well-to-do friends. Quite how the latter happened I'm still not entirely sure, but it made for a wonderful story.

What stayed with me wasn't necessarily any single anecdote. It was how easily two people from different generations, backgrounds and places could spend several hours enjoying each other's company. I don't have conversations like that very often, although I suspect Linda might.

I've met some fascinating people through the course too. During one conversation about my Yorkshire–Malta heritage, a gentleman told me about a Maltese man he knew who'd lived and worked in Sheffield as a rather daring window cleaner. Apparently, he called himself 'the Monkey of Sheffield', which I found particularly amusing.

The gentleman subsequently insisted on buying me lunch, at which point I introduced him to the expression 'there's no such thing as a free lunch'. He sniggered, although whether he genuinely found it funny or was simply being polite, I'm not sure.

I'm enjoying these encounters as much as the course itself. Perhaps being away from the usual routines makes it easier to spend time talking to people without thinking about what you should be doing next. Whatever the reason, it's been a refreshing part of the experience.

## Looking around

![An old limestone chapel in front of a much taller modern glass-fronted development in Malta](/images/malta-2026/malta-old-and-new.jpg)

I've also been exploring Gżira, where my Nanna was from, taking photographs of the streets and buildings. There's something about Malta's architecture that I find appealing, although it's certainly not all picturesque.

Beautiful old limestone houses with traditional balconies sit alongside modern apartment blocks and enormous glass-fronted developments. Some of the older buildings are well maintained, while others are weathered, boarded up or clearly showing their age. Construction seems to be taking place almost everywhere, and parking is apparently something of a competitive sport.

My initial impression has been of a country on the up, at least visually. There's clearly considerable development taking place, and some of the newer buildings suggest substantial investment. Interestingly, that hasn't necessarily been the impression I've taken from the people I've spoken to.

One of my new professors, Sandra Dingli, offered an interesting counterpoint during a conversation about Malta's development. While the economic numbers might look positive on paper, she explained, that doesn't necessarily reflect the experience of the people living here. Housing and everyday living costs have risen considerably, and the pace of development and population growth bring pressures of their own.

She suggested that perhaps the same could be said of many countries, including the UK.

It reminded me of something I encounter regularly through my work with data. We can measure economic performance, investment and growth, but those numbers don't necessarily tell us whether people are experiencing those changes as progress. It's quite possible for the picture presented by the numbers to look encouraging while the reality of everyday life feels rather different.

![Weathered limestone buildings on a Gżira street corner, with newer apartment buildings rising behind them](/images/malta-2026/malta-gzira-street.jpg)

Of course, I've only been here a few days, so I'm conscious that my impressions are those of someone visiting rather than living here. Still, I find the contrast interesting, particularly when walking through streets where the old and new seem to be competing for space.

There's something else I've been thinking about, too, and that's the question of scale. I've often thought of the UK as a relatively small country, but Malta puts that into perspective.

I heard during the week that Malta doesn't have a crematorium. I haven't checked all the detail behind that, but it's one of those peculiar things that makes you think about the size of a country in a way population figures don't.

Professor Dingli also told me about the Maltese enthusiasm for Eurovision. Apparently, it's a major occasion, with parties, streets becoming unusually quiet as people gather to watch, and the country collectively dreaming of winning.

I rather like that image. A small island nation getting behind its entry in a competition watched across Europe. And let's face it, they've probably got a better chance of winning than we have in the UK!

It makes me wonder how much the size of a country influences the way people experience belonging and national events. Perhaps there's something different about these occasions when the population is small enough for them to feel more collective. Or perhaps I'm simply looking for connections because that's what I've come here to learn to do.

Either way, it's interesting.

## A day off, almost

The first week of the Master's has been fascinating. I've met people from different backgrounds and disciplines, encountered ideas I'm keen to explore further, and already found myself thinking about things that weren't necessarily on the reading list.

One of the reasons I wanted to undertake the programme was to better understand my own thinking, how ideas emerge and how we connect things that might not initially seem related. There's plenty of work ahead on that front, but so far the experience of being here has been as interesting as the formal teaching.

I've come back to somewhere that feels familiar and found myself having all sorts of unfamiliar experiences. It's an interesting combination, and one I'm enjoying rather more than I expected.

Anyway, today I've got something resembling a day off, although there's a work call to get through first. After that, I'm planning to head into Valletta and take a boat across to the Three Cities.

Not a bad way to spend an October afternoon.
