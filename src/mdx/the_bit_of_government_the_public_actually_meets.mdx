I've been having a miserable time with a government service recently. There is a complaint under way, so I won't name the organisation or get into the merits of the case here. I'd rather write about the part that has got under my skin: how clear a public body can be about what it expects from you, and how hard it can be to find out what happens when you tell it something may be wrong.

At one point I received a reply that acknowledged my email, confirmed that the action against me would remain in place and directed me to the complaints process. I read it a couple of times because I couldn't quite work out what, if anything, my email had changed. There may be perfectly sound reasons for that answer. I don't have the full picture yet. From my end, though, the complaint and the action seemed able to carry on separately, with no explanation of how one might affect the other.

The language I'd been dealing with already felt unnecessarily aggressive. It is hard to separate that tone from the way the service works. If an organisation is confident and specific when it tells you what will happen if you don't act, you start to notice when it is much less specific about what *it* will do after you challenge something. A link to a complaints procedure tells you where to go next. It doesn't tell you whether anybody can actually change what is happening now.

I work in public service, which makes this uncomfortable in a useful way. My instinct is to start imagining the internal arrangement: perhaps one team handles correspondence, another handles enforcement and a third handles complaints. I have no idea whether that is true in this case, and I don't want to claim that nobody has looked properly at what I sent. But I recognise how easy it is for an organisation to describe each part of a process as working while the person on the receiving end is left trying to join those parts together.

There is probably a technology version of this story, about joining up systems or improving the flow of information. Those things might help. But first I would want to know something simpler: when a person says there may be a mistake, who can see the whole case and who can change course? What does the person get told while that is being considered? It is possible to make correspondence much faster without improving the answer to either of those questions.

I don't expect an enforcement process to stop automatically because someone disputes it. I do think a public body should be able to explain what happens to that process while a challenge is considered. It has powers the person on the other end of the letter doesn't have, and that ought to come with a serious obligation to check its own work.

My complaint is still open. The action is still in place. I don't yet know whether anyone dealing with one can affect the other.
