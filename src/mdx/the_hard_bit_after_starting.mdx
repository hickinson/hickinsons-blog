By Friday evening, the week already felt full.

There were mortgage emails and figures to check, the sort of paperwork that is both dull and consequential. We had plans to go out for a friend’s birthday. The weather had improved enough that sitting at a laptop for another few hours felt less appealing than it had in March.

Somewhere among all of that was the knowledge that Friday was supposed to mean another blog post.

I made a conscious decision not to write it.

Nothing dramatic happened. Nobody complained. There was no editor waiting for copy and no audience refreshing the homepage in disappointment. We went out. The mortgage moved closer to being sorted. Saturday arrived exactly as it would have done if I had spent the evening rearranging paragraphs.

It was a completely reasonable decision.

It also became easier to make again.

## The confidence of the first month

I had started the blog only six weeks earlier and published six pieces in fairly quick succession. That was enough to create the feeling that I had established something.

There was a website. There were categories. There was a Friday cadence. The archive had begun to fill. Each new article made the next one feel more likely.

But six repetitions are not much of a habit, particularly when each repetition involves finding an idea, deciding what it means, writing a coherent article and then being willing to put your name on it.

Starting had been carried by novelty. There was satisfaction in seeing a long-delayed idea finally exist. Even the technical parts were enjoyable: creating the file, checking the page, committing it to GitHub and watching it appear on the site.

The first month contained its own momentum.

It had not yet encountered much resistance.

The missed Friday was the first time the new routine had to compete properly with everything else. Not with laziness in any simple sense, but with work, friendship, family responsibility, financial administration and the understandable desire to spend less of life looking at a screen.

That distinction matters.

Advice about habits often treats every competing demand as a distraction to be overcome. Protect the time. Remove the friction. Do not break the chain.

There is some truth in that. A practice cannot survive if it is abandoned whenever something more attractive appears.

But life is not made entirely of distractions. Sometimes several worthwhile things ask for the same evening.

## The old habit was still stronger

Once I had consciously missed one Friday, the next decision changed slightly.

The perfect sequence had already gone. There was no longer an unbroken run to protect. The small discomfort of failing to publish had passed without consequence, which made postponement easier to justify the next time.

I had not formed a publishing habit. I had briefly established a publishing routine, and the much older default was still waiting underneath it.

That older default was not exactly *not writing*. I have always written in some form: notes, messages, project documents, half-finished arguments, long conversations, titles without articles and articles that existed mostly in my head.

What I had not done consistently was finish.

That became clearer over the months that followed. The blog went quiet, but the thinking did not.

Ideas accumulated through work on data strategy, local government reorganisation, AI-assisted projects, interviews, family, money, physical work at home and questions about what the next part of my career might look like. Some appeared in notes. Others developed through conversations. A few became detailed project prompts that were almost essays in disguise.

So it would be too simple to say that I stopped writing.

I stopped publishing.

That is not the same thing, although it can be comforting to pretend that it is.

Private thinking has value. Capturing an idea before it disappears has value. Talking something through until its shape becomes clearer has value.

But a folder full of fragments is not a body of published work. Neither is a long conversation that never survives outside the chat window. At some point, writing requires selection, structure, deletion and the mildly uncomfortable act of deciding that a thought is ready to leave your desk.

I had preserved the raw material while repeatedly avoiding that final step.

## A process can wait for you

The useful thing is that the first burst of activity had left something behind.

The site still existed. The repository still worked. The publication process was already known. A post needed a title, a date, a category and a description. The MDX file went into the same folder. The filename became the URL. A local build could check it before a commit and push sent it on its way.

None of that is especially sophisticated, which is probably why it survived.

I did not need to redesign the site, choose another platform or spend three weeks creating a better content system. The machinery was still sitting where I had left it.

More importantly, the writing had not vanished. It had accumulated messily across notes, conversations and lived experience. Restarting therefore did not mean manufacturing a new set of subjects or pretending the quiet months had been empty.

It meant recovering the trail.

That has made climbing back aboard surprisingly easy—he says, on the first day back aboard.

There is a useful lesson in that, although perhaps not the obvious one.

I had managed to build a publication process before I had built a publishing habit. The habit failed under pressure, but the process reduced the cost of returning. It held the door open while the writing continued elsewhere.

That does not absolve me from finishing the work. A good process can become another form of procrastination if I spend more time refining it than using it. Nor does a backlog of ideas guarantee that any of them deserve publication.

But it means the pause was not a complete reset.

## Designing for the return

Perhaps we think about habits too much in terms of uninterrupted streaks.

The real test of a practice may not be whether it survives every busy Friday. It may be whether there is a credible route back after life reasonably wins.

I do not regret going out for the birthday drinks. I certainly do not think the mortgage should have waited so that a lightly read personal blog could preserve its publication record.

The mistake, such as it was, came afterwards. I had no clear answer to what happened next.

There was no smaller version of the practice for difficult weeks. No field note instead of an essay. No scheduled return to an unfinished draft. No deliberate acceptance that a Friday could move without becoming three months.

I had designed the start and imagined the streak.

I had not designed the recovery.

The first missed Friday did not end the blog. It simply revealed that enthusiasm had been doing more of the work than I realised.

The process waited. The writing accumulated. And when I eventually came back, I did not have to begin again.

I only had to decide what to finish first.

Whether that makes the next Friday easier remains to be seen.
