I've been thinking about a slightly awkward problem for personalised AI: some of the things that matter most about a person may never have become data.

I saved a chapter recently called [*The carriers*](https://orphans.ai/03-the-carriers), by Doug Scott. He describes relationships built over years through small private exchanges, gestures, remembered acts and long periods where very little is said at all. Look only at the public record and much of what made those relationships meaningful simply isn't there.

In the follow-on chapter, [*The data problem*](https://orphans.ai/04-the-data-problem), he makes the argument more explicit. Some of this material is short, private, embedded in relationships and difficult to understand without the history around it.

That caught my attention because so much of the promise around increasingly personal AI can sound like a context problem. Give a system more information, connect more sources, preserve more memory and it can build a richer picture of us.

There is obviously something to that. A system with access to years of messages, documents, photographs, calendars and conversations has more to work with than one that knows only what somebody has put into the public domain.

But more context is still context that exists.

Most relationships are full of moments nobody thought to preserve. A joke that only makes sense to two people. The fact that somebody showed up. A tone of voice. A conversation neither person remembers accurately enough to reproduce. A three-word reply that carries twenty years of shared history with it.

Even when the message survives, the meaning may not.

That seems different from the usual problem of finding, organising or retrieving data. It made me wonder whether *missing data* is even quite the right description. Some of this material was never missing from a database. It was never in one.

It existed as experience, memory and relationship.

That distinction becomes interesting if we imagine AI systems becoming genuinely useful personal companions: able to remember what we have told them, search across our information and build a persistent model of what matters to us. They may become extremely good at reading the traces we leave behind.

The traces are still not the whole person.

That isn't an argument that AI cannot understand people, or that private human experience is somehow inaccessible to machines by definition. Plenty of things that were once ephemeral can now be recorded, and people may choose to give increasingly personal systems access to much more of their lives.

But there is a slightly strange implication if the answer is simply to capture everything.

Would recording every conversation, preserving every message and turning more of ordinary life into retrievable context actually solve the problem? Or would some of the value of these exchanges change precisely because they were no longer small, private and unrecorded?

I don't know.

What I do know is that a person leaves at least two kinds of trace. There is the material that can be found: the things they wrote, made, photographed, sent or stored. Then there is whatever remains in other people: what they remember, what they learned, what a particular sentence meant at a particular moment, why somebody still answers after ten years.

The second kind may occasionally become data later, when somebody tells the story. Much of it probably won't.

The harder question may not be how much context an AI needs. It may be what counts as context when some of the important stuff only exists between people.

I don't know how you put that in a context window.
